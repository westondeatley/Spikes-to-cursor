00 The hook

1,024 electrodes. One tiny radio.

An implant like Neuralink's records 1,024 channels, 20,000 times a second each. Its wireless link carries about one megabit per second. Before a computer can use the data, the implant has to throw away more than 99% of it.

In plain words

Your brain has about 86 billion . They talk to each other with tiny electrical pulses called , each lasting about a thousandth of a second. A places small next to neurons, listens for those spikes, and turns them into commands, such as moving a cursor for someone who is paralyzed.

Think of it like 1,024 microphones in a packed stadium, each catching a few nearby voices over the roar of the crowd. The implant has to work out what matters and send only that, through a link that carries about 1/200th of what the microphones record.

01 Raw signal & filtering

Where are the spikes?

One electrode hears everything nearby. A filter separates the fast spikes from the slow background.

In plain words

An electrode doesn't hear spikes cleanly. It records a mix: slow waves from thousands of distant neurons (the ), electrical hum from the building's wiring (), and random hiss. Spikes are short, sharp events, so they're made of fast wiggles, while most of the background is slow. A keeps the wiggle speeds () we want and removes the rest.

Think of it like the bass and treble knobs on a stereo. Turn the bass all the way down and the slow rumble disappears, leaving the sharp sounds.

A Butterworth filter of order N, built from small two-step pieces called biquads. Each output mixes the latest inputs and outputs. A filter only uses the past. The offline version runs the filter forward and then backward, which needs the future.

02 Detection & sorting

Which neuron fired?

Find every spike, then group them by shape. Because this recording is simulated, we can grade every step against the right answer.

In plain words

Finding spikes: draw a line below the noise. Any dip that crosses it counts as a spike. That line is the . Put it too close to the noise and random wiggles get counted. Put it too deep and small real spikes are missed.

Sorting spikes: every neuron's spike has its own , depending on the cell and how far it is from the electrode. We summarize each shape as a dot on a chart (), so similar shapes land close together. Then draws the groups.

Think of it like sorting a recording of a crowded room by voice: first find every moment someone spoke, then group the moments by who sounds alike.

    03 Decoding intent

    Reading a movement from neurons

    Move your mouse inside the workspace. Simulated neurons fire as if you intended that movement, and a decoder rebuilds the cursor from their spikes alone.

    In plain words

    In the , the part of the brain that controls movement, many neurons have a . One fires fastest when you intend to move right, another when you intend to move up and to the left. A single neuron is too noisy to trust, but hundreds together are reliable. A is the math that combines them into a cursor velocity.

    Before it can do that, the decoder has to learn each neuron's favorite direction. That happens during : the person watches a cursor move to targets and imagines moving it.

    Think of it like asking a crowd to point toward a sound. Each person is a bit off, but the average of hundreds of pointing arms is very accurate.

    The math optional

    B = log2(N − 1) × max(0, Sc − Si) / t

    N squares, Sc correct and Si incorrect selections in t seconds: the achieved bitrate (Nuyujukian et al., 2015). Neuralink reports that its clinical trial participants have exceeded 10 bits per second on a 35 × 35 grid.

    The twist to discuss

    The Kalman filter scored best in Stage 3, yet many players do better here with the simpler decoders. Its smoothing makes it trail your intention by about a tenth of a second, and when a person is steering, that delay causes overshoot. Being accurate on recorded data (offline) is not the same as being easy to control (online). Researchers fixed this by recalibrating the Kalman filter on data recorded while the person was using it, an approach called ReFIT (Gilja et al., 2012).

    05 Wrap-up

    What's still hard

    You just ran the whole chain, from a noisy voltage to a cursor you could control. Each step leaves open problems that companies and labs are working on now.

    1. 00RecordFar more data than a tiny radio can send, so it must shrink on the chip.
    2. 01FilterKeep the fast wiggles where spikes live, in real time.
    3. 02Detect & sortA threshold finds spikes; shape tells neurons apart, when it can.
    4. 03DecodeMany noisy neurons vote; the Kalman filter adds memory of motion.
    5. 04ControlA person in the loop corrects the decoder's mistakes, scored in bits per second.

    Signals drift

    You saw it in Stage 3: by day 30, a decoder fitted on day 0 explains much less of the movement. Clinical systems recalibrate often, or keep adapting quietly while the person uses them.

    Power and heat

    The whole implant, radio included, has to run on under 10 milliwatts so it doesn't warm the brain. Every algorithm on the chip must be simple and efficient, which is why compression and spike detection matter so much.

    Electrodes age

    The body treats electrodes as foreign objects. Scar tissue can build up around them and weaken the signals over months to years. Thin, flexible threads are one attempt to reduce this.

    Beyond the cursor

    The same pipeline with different decoders has been used in research to decode attempted handwriting and speech, aiming to restore communication for people who can't speak.

    Questions to leave with

    If you could only send 1% of the data, which 1% would you keep? Should the decoder adapt to the brain, or should the brain learn the decoder? Who should own the data an implant records?
